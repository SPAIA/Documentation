# MAX30102 insect motion trigger experiments

Status: outline. Items marked TODO need dates, numbers or photos from the original test runs.

## 1. Summary

- What we tried: using the MAX30102 heart rate sensor as a motion trigger for an ESP32 camera, to photograph insects.
- Result in one line: it detects movement reliably on the bench, but the detection range is too short to be useful in the field.
- Recommendation (keep, drop, or use only in a narrow case). TODO

## 2. Background and goal

- The problem. We want to capture insect photos without a camera recording all the time, which wastes power, storage and bandwidth.
- Why a trigger is needed. TODO (power budget, image volume, false captures)
- The idea. A sensor that reports "something moved in front of the camera" and wakes the capture.

## 3. Why the MAX30102

- What it is. Pulse oximeter and heart rate sensor with an IR LED, a red LED and a photodetector.
- The theory. An insect in front of the sensor changes the amount of reflected IR light, so the signal changes when something moves.
- Features that made it attractive.
  - Built-in ambient light cancellation.
  - Filtering that rejects UV and most visible light.
  - Small, cheap, I2C, easy to pair with an ESP32.
- What we tried before it. In 2020 we tried detecting insects from the camera image itself (repo `playstate_africa/camera-detection` on Bitbucket, Jan to Aug 2020). Full write-up in [early_camera_experiments.md](early_camera_experiments.md).
  - First version (Jan 2020). The ESP32-CAM captures grayscale QVGA frames. The code averages brightness in five concentric bands, 2 pixels wide, working inward from the frame edge. When the band with the biggest change between frames passes a threshold of 2.2 and the active band moves inward or outward, it prints INCOMING or OUTGOING. The idea was to spot an insect entering the frame and tell which way it was moving.
  - Later version (Feb to Aug 2020, `develop` branch). Switched to a grid of cells on 160x120 grayscale frames and published the per-cell averages over MQTT with TLS, apparently for analysis off the device. The on-device detection function (`findBugs`) is declared but never written.
  - Why it was dropped. Averaging brightness was not accurate enough to reliably detect smaller insects, shadows triggered it easily, and running the camera constantly used too much power. A separate IR sensor that uses very little power and responds to nearby reflection, not overall light, addressed all three.
- Other alternatives considered, such as PIR, ultrasonic or break-beam sensors. TODO say which ones we ruled out and why.

## 4. Hardware setup

- Components (from the `field_agent` firmware).
  - ESP32-CAM (PlatformIO board `esp32cam`, PSRAM enabled).
  - MAX30102 breakout, driven through the SparkFun MAX3010x library (the code calls it MAX30101 / MAX30105, which share a register map with the MAX30102).
  - RV1805 real-time clock, for timestamps.
  - BME280, for temperature and humidity at each capture.
  - SD card (SD_MMC), for storing images and a CSV of events.
  - Wi-Fi, set up through a WiFiManager captive portal, for uploading events and images to the SPAIA API.
  - Power source. TODO
- Wiring. All three I2C sensors share one bus, SDA on GPIO 12 and SCL on GPIO 13.
- Physical arrangement. Sensor position relative to the lens, enclosure, any window or lens over the sensor. TODO photo
- Sensor settings, v0.2 firmware (Oct 2021 to Jan 2022).
  - LED brightness 0xFF (maximum, about 50 mA). A code comment says the aim was to "sense up to 18 inches".
  - Red and green LED amplitude set to 0, so IR only.
  - Sample rate 400, sample averaging 8, pulse width 411, ADC range 2048 (the most sensitive range).
  - I2C on GPIO 14 and 15, with a DS1307 RTC.
- Sensor settings, final firmware (2022 to 2023).
  - LED brightness 0xFF again.
  - Red and IR LEDs both on, but only IR is used for detection.
  - Sample rate 1600, sample averaging 32, pulse width 411, ADC range 16384.
  - Check this combination against the datasheet. As far as I know the chip only supports 1600 samples per second at the shortest pulse width, so it may have been clamped to a lower rate. TODO confirm what the sensor actually ran at.
  - I2C moved to GPIO 12 and 13, with an RV1805 RTC.

## 5. Firmware and trigger logic

- Code lives in two private repos.
  - `playstate_africa/0.02` on Bitbucket. The v0.2 firmware, 15 commits from 20 Oct 2021 to 17 Jan 2022. Everything is in `src/main.cpp`.
  - https://github.com/SPAIA/field_agent. The later firmware, April 2022 to June 2023. The detector is `lib/photo_detector/MAX30101.cpp` and the main loop is `src/main.cpp`.
- How the v0.2 detector works (`bugHunt()` in the Bitbucket repo).
  - At power-up it averages 40 IR readings and keeps that as a fixed baseline.
  - It fires when a reading is more than `IRThreshold` above the baseline. The default is 50 counts. Only increases count, which fits the theory that a nearby insect reflects more IR.
  - After a capture it waits at least 1 second before it can fire again.
  - The threshold can be changed from the server. The device downloads `IRThreshold` along with its sleep times and stores them in EEPROM, so sensitivity could be tuned without reflashing.
  - Duty cycle. The device stays awake for 10 seconds, hunting, then deep sleeps for 5 seconds. The sensor and camera are shut down while it sleeps, so nothing can trigger during that time. TODO check how much this cost us in missed insects.
  - Wi-Fi connects on first boot and then every 10th boot, to fetch settings, check for a firmware update (served from DigitalOcean Spaces) and upload the CSV and images to the SPAIA API.
  - A fixed baseline taken once at power-up drifts as light and temperature change. That may be why the later firmware switched to a running average. TODO confirm with whoever wrote it.
- How the later detector works (`SPAIA_MAX::hunt()` in `field_agent`).
  - Reads each new IR sample from the sensor FIFO and compares it with a running average.
  - The average covers a window of 10 samples. At the end of the window it restarts from the last average.
  - A trigger needs two steps. The signal must first be "settled", meaning the difference stayed under the threshold after at least 10 samples. Then a sample that differs from the average by more than the threshold fires the trigger.
  - Threshold is 5 raw ADC counts, which is very small. TODO say whether this was tuned or left from early testing, and what the noise floor looked like.
  - There is no cooldown or debounce beyond the settle step.
  - A red channel check exists but is commented out, so only IR drives detection.
- What happens on a trigger (`loop()` in `main.cpp`).
  1. Shut the sensor down, so its LEDs don't interfere with the camera.
  2. Read the RTC and fire the flash.
  3. Capture two images 50 ms apart, named `<chipID>-<time>_A` and `_B`.
  4. Read temperature and humidity from the BME280.
  5. Write a row to the CSV on the SD card.
  6. Upload the CSV and images if the device is on Wi-Fi.
  7. Wake the sensor and go back to hunting.
- Power. The loop polls continuously. The deep sleep function in `main.cpp` is defined but never called, so the device does not sleep between triggers. TODO confirm this was also true in the field units, and what battery life we got.
- Camera setup. A startup routine cycles auto settings and the flash. Commit history mentions switching camera and "taking decent pictures inside". TODO which camera module we ended on.

## 9. What went wrong

- Main finding. The sensor only triggers when the insect is very close, so most insects near the camera never set it off.
- Why. Reflected IR drops off quickly with distance, and the sensor is designed for skin contact, not open air.
- Secondary issues. Wind-moved vegetation, sunlight, small insects with low reflectivity. TODO confirm which of these we saw
- Why the bench result did not carry over. The test targets were bigger, closer and more reflective than real insects at a realistic distance.
